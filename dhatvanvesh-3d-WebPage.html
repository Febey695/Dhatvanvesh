How it works

The lander is a probe on a wire. The vessel supplies power and carries the data; the lander carries only the sensors, a small computer and the ballast that gets it to the bottom. Every drop follows the same four-part chain.

  1. Drop. Crew lower the lander over the side at a waypoint chosen from the ship's bathymetry.
  2. Descend. Ballast takes it down at about 1 m/s; the tether pays out freely.
  3. Land and level. Legs settle, the IMU confirms tilt, the boom lowers the coil to 30 cm above the seabed.
  4. Screen. Magnetometer and EM coil sweep, the camera counts nodules, probes log temperature, ORP and pH.
  5. Score. The on-board model classifies the station: nodules, sulphides, crust, REE mud or barren.
  6. Recover and repeat. Winch back or release the ballast, move to the next waypoint. Each station adds a point to the heat map.

Step 1 of 5

Released over the side, mid-survey

The lander is lowered from the vessel's A-frame on a fibre-optic tether. No ROV, no dedicated cruise: it rides along on a survey that is already happening and is dropped wherever the bathymetry looks promising.

Two crew and a winch are enough to deploy it.

Step 2 of 5

Free descent, ballast-assisted

Steel ballast and a compact frame take it down at roughly a metre per second. The tether carries power and a live data link, so the pressure housing needs only a small battery and no acoustic modem.

Rated for abyssal depths where nodule fields sit, typically 4,000 to 5,500 m.

Step 3 of 5

Touchdown and self-levelling

Three legs settle into the sediment. The IMU checks tilt, then the sensor boom swings the induction coil and magnetometer to a fixed height above the seabed so readings are comparable from site to site.

Step 4 of 5

A minute of screening, four ways

Fluxgate magnetometer
Picks up magnetic anomalies from iron- and cobalt-rich crusts and sulphide bodies.
EM induction coil
Measures seabed conductivity; metallic nodules and sulphides stand out sharply from sediment.
Camera and LED array
Counts and sizes nodules on the surface with on-board image analysis.
Temperature, ORP and pH
Flags hydrothermal activity and the chemistry that goes with rare-earth-bearing muds.
Step 5 of 5

Readings become a map

Each drop returns a scored point: metal likelihood, deposit type and a photo. Repeated drops along the survey line build a heat map the ship's team can act on the same day, choosing where a full sampling programme is worth the cost.

  • Polymetallic nodules — high conductivity, visible in imagery
  • Hydrothermal sulphides — magnetic and thermal anomaly together
  • Cobalt-rich crusts — strong magnetic signal on hard substrate
  • REE-bearing sediments — chemistry and fine-sediment signature
